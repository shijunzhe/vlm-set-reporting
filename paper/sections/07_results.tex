\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The results follow the three problems of Section~\ref{sec:intro}: a comparison with common practice (Section~\ref{sec:res:common}), repairing reports (Sections~\ref{sec:res:readout} to \ref{sec:res:view}), detecting errors (Section~\ref{sec:res:persist}), repairing a click by choosing among several models, confirmed on new screenshots (Section~\ref{sec:res:choose}), and judging which report or configuration is more accurate (Sections~\ref{sec:res:select} and \ref{sec:res:transfer}). Each table caption states the operation, the comparator, the loss and any label use.

\subsection{Against common practice}\label{sec:res:common}

\input{tables/t7_common}

Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c and Table~\ref{tab:choose} summarise the result with the widest scope: on 600 ScreenSpot-Pro items not used in designing it, the several-model click tier misses 9.33\% of targets against 13.67\% for GPT-6.1's two-stage zoom and 12.67\% for three such zooms at about the same cost (Section~\ref{sec:res:choose}). The comparisons below use the stored calls of the earlier experiments.

Table~\ref{tab:common} compares the defaults of Section~\ref{sec:method} with the remedies practitioners use most, on the same stored calls. The pattern follows the three problems. Self-consistency repeats the same source of evidence, so its gains are small: the median of three stated counts helps GPT-6.1 by 23\% and the other two models by about 1\%, at three times the cost, and a vote of three clicks changes click error by $-4.5\%$ to $+2.5\%$. Uncertainty-gated zoom, which zooms only when two clicks of the same model disagree, inherits the same weakness, because persistent errors rarely produce disagreement; it gains 13\% for Gemini 3.8 and nothing significant for the other two. The remedies that change the information win: reading the list cuts dense-count error by 85\% and 99\% for about the cost of one call, and zooming every item cuts click error by 45\% and 65\% for less than a three-click vote. For set error, three-call unanimity with fused coordinates improves 9 of the 15 over-reporting cells (median $-9.6\%$), against $-2.9\%$ for majority vote and $-3.7\%$ for box fusion, and it beats box fusion directly in 11 of 18 cells and is not significantly worse in any. For detecting click errors at the same distance threshold, a second model catches 1.9 to 3.5 times the share of errors that a second call of the same model catches, while flagging more items (Section~\ref{sec:res:persist}).

\subsection{Repair the readout: read the list, not the number}\label{sec:res:readout}

\paragraph{Same response, two answers.} Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows one response in full. When a list-first response has both a list and stated counts, the list length misses the true total by 0.04 objects on average for GPT-6.1 and 0.02 for Sonnet 5.5, while the stated totals miss by 7.78 and 10.48 (Table~\ref{tab:readout}, last row). Reading per-type counts from the list (the list count) instead of the stated counts in the same response cuts per-type count error by 75.6\% (GPT-6.1) and 77.7\% (Sonnet 5.5) under a count-first prompt, and by 84.2\% and 70.7\% under the list-first prompt. Generation is held fixed in these comparisons, so the gain is a readout effect: the model's list carries information that its own summary loses. Proposition~\ref{prop:readout} adds that the size of such a gap shows the two readouts are inconsistent but does not by itself say which one is right; here the labels say it is the list.

\paragraph{The gap grows with the length of a multi-type list.} Figure~\ref{fig:density} (values in Table~\ref{tab:density}) repeats the same-response comparison on four datasets. On PixMo (6 objects per image, one class) and FSC-147 (43.8 objects, one class), the stated total tracks the list for GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 and the difference is not significant. On SCP (47.6 objects, five classes) the list count is better by 44\% for GPT-6.1 and by 98.5\% for Sonnet 5.5, whose stated per-type counts are 65 times as far from the truth as its list. On SCP-dense (164.3 objects) both models gain 76\% to 78\%. The gap therefore appears when the stated count must summarize a long list across several classes.

\paragraph{Aliases and protocol.} In list-first responses, Sonnet 5.5 wrote 258 ``diamond'' records across 50 images although the format listed the allowed names. The declared alias map, applied to the same list, takes its error from 5.42 to 0.42 ($-92.3\%$). The prompt alone does not do this: the map is part of the task, and the decoder applies it. Holding the mapped readout fixed, list-first lists are better than count-first lists by 39.4\% for GPT-6.1 and 92.3\% for Sonnet 5.5. The complete protocol, compared with the stated count of a count-first response, cuts error by 85.2\% $[-89.1,-80.3]$ for GPT-6.1 and 98.8\% $[-99.5,-98.1]$ for Sonnet 5.5. For Gemini 3.8 at its lowest setting the change is $+15.6\%$ $[-5.3,+39.4]$, not significant: the degeneracy check routes 40 of its 50 responses to the stated count. These are effects of the reporting and decoding protocol. The same-response and same-list comparisons isolate the readout and the alias map; the full protocol also changes how the list is generated, so it does not establish a change in perceptual ability either way.

\paragraph{Aerial and document images with several classes.} The readout repair needs a gap to work with. We ran both prompts on 30 DOTA windows and 50 DocLayNet pages chosen to contain several classes and many objects (Table~\ref{tab:natural}; 574 calls). There the lists themselves miss objects: GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 list 68\% to 88\% of the true objects. The stated count also stays close to the list count of the same response, differing by 0.1 to 7.4 per image against per-type errors of 5 to 46. Switching readouts can change the error by at most this gap (Proposition~\ref{prop:readout}), so the change in the same response is small: none for GPT-6.1, and $-13.5\%$ and $-19.9\%$ for Sonnet 5.5. The full list-first protocol, here without the recovery step, changes count error by $+0.4\%$ and $-2.4\%$ for GPT-6.1 and by $-15.7\%$ and $-20.1\%$ for Sonnet 5.5, intervals reaching zero. Gemini 3.8 at its lowest setting again writes degenerate lists; the fallback to the stated count holds its change to $+18.0\%$ and $-7.9\%$, both not significant, and at its default setting, the shipped configuration, it changes count error by $-28.2\%$ on DOTA (interval reaching zero) and by $-25.8\%$ on DocLayNet. The two kinds of scene differ in where the error sits. On dense synthetic scenes the lists are nearly complete and the statement loses information, so the readout repairs most of the error; on these aerial and document images most of the error is already in the list, so switching between the two readouts cannot remove it. The readout gap, which needs no labels, separates the two cases in one direction: a small gap shows that switching between the stated count and the list count can change the error by at most the gap, while a large gap shows only that the switch might matter. The gap says nothing about remedies that produce a new response.

\subsection{Repair the readout when the list degenerates}\label{sec:res:degen}

\input{tables/t2_setting}

Gemini 3.8 at its lowest setting writes long lists in which most records lack a valid class and some coordinates repeat, so the list count is far worse than its stated count ($+182.5\%$ in the same response; Figure~\ref{fig:density}). The four-part degeneracy check of Section~\ref{sec:method} flags 45 of its 50 dense-scene lists. For 40 of them the readout falls back to the stated count; the other 5 have no stated count and keep the list count with a status flag. On the GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 list-first responses it flags none.

For Gemini 3.8, degeneration depends mainly on the reasoning setting. At the same 16k output cap, Gemini 3.8's default setting reduces flagged lists from 90\% to 12\% and the readout error from 52.80 to 24.52 ($-53.6\%$ $[-72.4,-29.5]$; Table~\ref{tab:setting}). Doubling the cap to 32k gives a point estimate of 15.44, a further change that is not significant. Even then, Gemini 3.8's dense-count error is about ten times GPT-6.1's, and none of its 50 counts is exact. Strict JSON parsing fails for 70\% to 78\% of Gemini 3.8 responses at every setting, so the declared record salvage of Section~\ref{sec:setup:contract} is a necessary part of the decoder, and its share is reported.

On the list-first responses of the readout experiment, applying the same check to existing responses recovers 68\% (SCP) and 58\% (SCP-dense) of Gemini 3.8's error relative to reading its list, and 42\% relative to the stated count on FSC-147. For Sonnet 5.5, whose lists are not degenerate, the cost of the check reaches $+23\%$ on SCP and $+13\%$ on SCP-dense (not significant; intervals $[0,57]$ and $[0,26]$). On the open model Qwen3-VL, with thresholds unchanged, it lowers SCP-dense error by 7\% $[-13,-1]$ relative to the stated count. Gemini 3.8's missing class fields follow a pattern: a record is more likely to omit its type when the previous record has the same type, and a row-by-row scan that interleaves types cuts its count error by 60\%, while grouping by type makes it worse.

\subsection{Repair the set: delete below the threshold, keep geometry separate}\label{sec:res:set}

\input{tables/t3_intersect}

\paragraph{Deletion and geometry, measured separately.} Table~\ref{tab:intersect} (per-pair values in Table~\ref{tab:intersect_full}) splits the effect of confirming a base call with a partner call into the selection term of Proposition~\ref{prop:identity} and the geometry term of Proposition~\ref{prop:geometry}, for 54 base/partner pairs on six sources. Both identities hold exactly on every image, so the selection term of a pair has the sign of $1-2p_1$ by construction (Figure~\ref{fig:select}a). What the data add is where $p_1$ lies: 0.00 to 0.39 on SKU-110K and 0.05 to 0.22 on DOTA, where deletion removes 4 to 68 errors per image, against 0.39 to 0.90 on FSC-147. On FSC-147, where models under-report, $p_1$ is 0.39 to 0.90 and deletion hurts in 7 of 9 pairs, which is why counting families keep a single call. Coordinate averaging is positive in 37 pairs and negative in 13. In two pairs it reverses the sign of the deletion-only result: DocLayNet with GPT-6.1 confirmed by Sonnet 5.5 (selection $+0.80$, geometry $-1.10$ per image) and GroundCUA with Sonnet 5.5 confirmed by itself (selection $-0.87$, geometry $+1.20$). These are the two pairs in which an earlier check of the threshold on fused intersections failed; separating the terms accounts for both.

\paragraph{The threshold from a few labels.} The useful question is whether $p_1$ measured on a handful of labelled images predicts what deletion will do on other images. For each pair we drew 5 labelled images 200 times, estimated $p_1$ on them, and predicted from Proposition~\ref{thm:intersect} whether deleting unconfirmed records lowers set error on the other 55 images of the pair. The prediction is right in 83\% of draws (80\% with 3 labelled images, 87\% with 10), and the loss against the better decision on the other images is 0.33 errors per image (0.48 and 0.15). The shipped regime alone, which deletes on over-reporting families and keeps on FSC-147 without any label, is right in 85\% of draws with a loss of 0.43. The label rule of Step 7 combines the two: it starts from the regime and switches only if the labelled images favour the other decision by more than one standard error. It is right in 86\%, 87\% and 88\% of draws with 3, 5 and 10 labelled images, with losses of 0.22, 0.17 and 0.10 errors per image. In the 8 pairs whose $p_1$ lies within 0.1 of $1/2$, the gain from deleting is small and its sign is noisy: the plain threshold is right in 47\% of draws there, and the regime and the Step 7 rule, which lean towards deletion, in 34\% and 37\%. Table~\ref{tab:intersect} gives the share by source.

\paragraph{Which partner helps.} Proposition~\ref{prop:partner} says a partner helps if it rejects more of the base call's false records than it loses of the true ones. Cross-model partners separate the two better than same-model partners in 32 of 36 base/partner comparisons on the E3 and screen-parsing sources. The four exceptions are GPT-6.1's two much weaker partners on FSC-147, and Gemini 3.8 confirming Sonnet 5.5 on FSC-147 and SKU-110K, where Gemini 3.8 confirms few of Sonnet 5.5's true records. The misses are consistent with a component shared across models: of the objects that GPT-6.1, the highest-recall model, misses in all three of its calls, a single call of either other model recovers only 0.7\% to 9\% on SKU-110K, DOTA and FSC-147, and 18\% to 26\% on DocLayNet. New information flows mainly from higher-recall to lower-recall models.

\subsection{Repair the view; fixes that do not help}\label{sec:res:view}

\paragraph{Zoom and tiles.} Two-stage zoom clicks once on the whole screenshot, crops a third of each side around that point at native pixels, and clicks again in the crop; it is a re-grounding strategy in the style of the ReGround method that \citet{li2025screenspotpro} evaluate. On ScreenSpot-Pro (200 items) it raises the click hit rate from 64.5\% to 87.5\% for Gemini 3.8 ($+23$ points $[16.5,29.5]$, a 65\% cut in click error) and from 78\% to 87\% for Sonnet 5.5 ($+9$ $[5,13.5]$, a 41\% cut; 45\% on the 196 items that every arm of Table~\ref{tab:common} answered); for GPT-6.1, 88.5\% to 90.0\% is not significant. Zooming only when two different models disagree, a cross-model version of the gated zoom in Table~\ref{tab:common}, is less accurate than zooming every item for Sonnet 5.5 (2.3 to 2.7 points) and Gemini 3.8 (9.6 to 12.3 points), not significantly different for GPT-6.1, and more expensive. Our tiling arm on FSC-147 used $2\times2$ crops that overlap by 10\% of the side, each enlarged $2\times$ and sent in a separate call. It raises the tokens per object side for every object, but its overlap is smaller than one object for the larger objects (median exemplar side 17.5\% of the image side in the largest third), so it does not meet the companion paper's safe-decomposition condition that every target lie inside some view \citep{shi2026zoomin}. The results follow that condition. On the smallest third of images, Sonnet 5.5's count error falls by 70\% against whole-image lists; on the largest third, where many objects lie wholly inside no crop, GPT-6.1's rises by 78\%; over all images Sonnet 5.5's change ($-21\%$, $[-50,+27]$) is not significant. The small-object gain mixes more tokens per object with less content per call, and the large-object loss is consistent with objects that no crop contains rather than with object size as such. On screens, where tiles overlap by 6\%, objects cut by a tile boundary lose 0.16 to 0.42 in report probability in all 6 cells, and separate calls per tile beat one call with several tiles for GPT-6.1 ($-15\%$, $[-23,-5]$). For native-resolution click crops, downscaling lowers Sonnet 5.5's hit rate by 9 points $[3,16]$, the loss for GPT-6.1 reaches 3 points ($[0,7]$, not significant), Gemini 3.8 shows no significant response to resizing, consistent with the fixed token grid measured for it in the companion paper, and enlarging these crops does not help.

\paragraph{Reports from a view planner.} In the crop calls of the Review experiment (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}) the pipeline receives, for each image, a whole-image report and four reports from four native-resolution crops (6\% overlap, one call per crop), on 30 GroundCUA screens and 30 SKU-110K images, scored at IoU $\ge0.5$ without class. Merging the four crop reports alone lowers set error relative to one whole-image call for GPT-6.1 on screens ($-19.4\%$ $[-27.5,-10.6]$) and for Sonnet 5.5 on SKU-110K ($-64.0\%$ $[-70.6,-56.6]$), raises it for Sonnet 5.5 on screens ($+44.9\%$) and for Gemini 3.8 on SKU-110K ($+24.7\%$), and is not significant in the other two cells. Confirming the whole-image report with the merged crops under the shipped rule, in which a crop confirms or rejects only the records it contains wholly, lowers set error in 4 of 6 cells: GPT-6.1 ($-22.8\%$) and Gemini 3.8 ($-20.3\%$) on screens, and GPT-6.1 ($-45.5\%$) and Sonnet 5.5 ($-36.6\%$) on SKU-110K. In these 4 cells it is also better than confirmation by a second whole-image call ($-11.8\%$ to $-22.6\%$), and better than the shipped three-call unanimity in 3 of them ($-8.0\%$ to $-20.2\%$; Gemini 3.8 on screens $-4.7\%$, not significant), at the price of five calls instead of three. In the other two cells it raises set error: Sonnet 5.5 on screens ($+12.9\%$) and Gemini 3.8 on SKU-110K ($+37.9\%$). The threshold of Proposition~\ref{thm:intersect} accounts for both groups. Among the whole-image records that the crops do not confirm, the share matched to a true object ($p_1$) is 0.03 to 0.24 in the four cells where confirmation helps, and 0.62 and 0.76 in the two where it hurts; this follows from an identity and is not an independent test. The plain rule, in which every record needs confirmation, differs from the shipped rule by at most 4 percentage points, because with a 6\% overlap few records lie wholly inside no crop.

\paragraph{Grids and coordinates.} On SCP, the systematic coordinate offset of current models is at most 0.3 pixels, with slope 1.000 and spread 0.9 to 1.5 pixels, against a matching tolerance of 30 pixels. A grid, ticks or cell labels therefore have no coordinate error to correct: in every arm, localization changes by at most 0.23 pixels, and at the lowest setting they do not change detection for GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5. For Gemini 3.8 at its lowest setting, a grid lowers set error by 3.5 per image by reducing records that omit their class, and a row-by-row scan lowers it by 11.5; at its default setting the grid makes detection and localization slightly worse. ``No overlay'' is therefore the shipped default, chosen from these measurements; the theory does not forbid an overlay from helping a model whose list format it changes. Markers also make Sonnet 5.5 write ``diamond'' more often ($+0.3$ per image). An overlay on the sent image adds no Shannon information and can only change how the model uses its input \citep{shi2026zoomin}. We saw two such changes, fewer omitted class fields for Gemini 3.8 and more uses of the word ``diamond'' for Sonnet 5.5, and no coordinate correction, because on SCP the offsets are about 1\% of the tolerance.

\paragraph{Wording, instructions and review.} ``Do not miss any'' wording, compared with a symmetric instruction not to miss or invent, has no measurable effect on SCP for any of the three models. Asking the model to localize each item relative to structures in the image has no significant effect, because current models have almost no location misses on these scenes. Same-view self-review, in which the model edits its own draft, confirms true and false records at nearly the same rate ($h_+\approx h_-$) and has no net gain on screen parsing; on FSC-147 it gave significant gains for 2 of 3 models, which we do not explain. Pairwise comparators that ask a model which of two reports is right judge whether something is an object rather than whether the box is precise, and are not used. Reasoning is not uniformly helpful: on 20 GroundCUA screens, GPT-6.1's default setting raises set error by 18.7 per screen $[5.8,34.3]$, through 16.3 additional false records and no fewer misses.

\subsection{Detect errors: they persist, so ask someone else}\label{sec:res:persist}

\paragraph{Persistence.} Object-level errors repeat across calls. Intraclass correlations of reporting across repeated calls are 0.60 to 0.98 for true objects and 0.23 to 0.93 for false reports. On the E3 sources, a binomial model with a common reporting probability is rejected in all 12 dataset and model cells, while a beta-binomial model fits. The share of misses that recur in every repeated call is 0.76 to 0.99 on the E3 sources and 0.73 to 0.94 on the screen sources. In clicks, 64\% to 83\% of a model's errors are repeated by a second call of the same model within the agreement tolerance.

\paragraph{A second model is a better alarm.} Persistence means that a second call of the same model rarely disagrees with the first where the first is wrong. A second model shares only part of the propensity. With the partner set by a fixed rule as the cheapest other model, the share of errors in which both calls agree on the same wrong point falls from 83\% to 39\% for GPT-6.1, from 64\% to 29\% for Sonnet 5.5, and from 73\% to 48\% for Gemini 3.8. An alarm defers an item when two points for it are farther apart than $\delta$ of the longer image side; \emph{coverage} is the share of items acted on, and the \emph{miss rate when acting} is the share of acted-on items whose point misses the target. At $\delta=1.5\%$, the same-model alarm catches 17\% of GPT-6.1's errors (4 of 23) and the cross-model alarm catches 61\% (14 of 23); for Sonnet 5.5 the shares are 36\% and 71\% (Table~\ref{tab:alarm}). The cross-model alarm defers more items, and Figure~\ref{fig:alarm} shows the trade-off: where the two curves overlap, its miss rate at matched coverage is lower by 0.5 to 2.7 points (point estimates on 260 items, without intervals; for GPT-6.1 at 93.1\% coverage it catches 9 of the 23 errors against 6 for the same-model alarm), and for GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 it reaches operating points with a miss rate of 3\% to 4\%, below the lowest same-model values (7.0\% and 10.5\%). After two-stage zoom the same pattern holds, so the advantage comes from the second model and not from zoom alone. We zoomed each model's second click as well (772 calls), so that both alarms compare zoomed points. At $\delta=1.5\%$ a second zoomed call of the same model catches 40\% to 50\% of the remaining errors, and the partner's zoomed point catches 68\% to 78\%, with miss rates of 2.6\% to 3.6\% on the 85\% to 88\% of items acted on (Table~\ref{tab:alarm}). The same-model alarm again acts on more items (93\% to 94\%); where the two curves overlap, the cross-model miss rate at matched coverage is lower by 0 to 1.5 points.

\paragraph{Re-asking a failed call.} Consistent with persistent errors, re-asking also rarely repairs a failed call: in the detection experiments, resending the same request cleared 14.7\% of status flags that had already repeated, while changing the request cleared 58.8\% (Step 3 of Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Repair a click by choosing among models, on new screenshots}\label{sec:res:choose}

\input{tables/t9_choose}

The several-model tier of Section~\ref{sec:method:clicks} was designed on the 260 click items of the GUI experiment, where it missed 11 targets against 16 for GPT-6.1's two-stage zoom at its default setting. We then ran it, with its code, prompts, judge and tie rule unchanged and recorded by hash, on 600 further ScreenSpot-Pro items that share no item and no screenshot with those 260, together with the baselines of Table~\ref{tab:choose}. All 13,120 requests completed (13,126 attempts with retries; \$44.93 at list prices for all arms), no flow needed the fallbacks for a failed call, zoom, score or comparison, and every item stays in the denominator. When the run was interrupted once and resumed, at most 8 requests were in flight without a stored response; their cost, at most about \$0.05, is not in this total. Single-model flows sometimes returned no point, counted as a miss (Table~\ref{tab:choose}); the three-model flows always returned one.

\paragraph{The tier against single-model practice.} On the 600 new items the tier misses 56 targets (9.33\%), against 82 (13.67\%) for GPT-6.1's two-stage zoom at its default setting: $-4.33$ points $[-6.33,-2.33]$, with 34 items repaired and 8 broken (exact McNemar $p=7\times10^{-5}$). At about the same cost, three independent default zooms of GPT-6.1 with a cluster vote miss 76, so the tier is better by $3.33$ points $[1.50,5.17]$ (Holm-adjusted $p=0.002$). Against a crop-first baseline that we implemented in the style of GUI-Lens \citep{fu2026guilens}, without its OCR and detector anchors or its verification step and with our own prompts, the tier misses 56 against 69: the difference is $-2.17$ points with an interval that reaches zero ($[-4.17,0.00]$; Holm-adjusted $p=0.060$), at 2.3 times that baseline's cost. The data therefore do not show an advantage over crop-first prompting. The comparisons support the tier as a whole; they do not by themselves attribute its gain to the diversity of its models.

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} Proposition~\ref{prop:three} splits the tier's 56 misses into 43 items on which no model's zoomed point hits ($F$), 3 on which a correct point exists but no cluster representative is correct ($L$), and 10 on which the comparison picks the wrong representative ($W$). With the judge's score alone, $W$ is 15 and the other two terms are unchanged. The comparison therefore repairs 8 items and breaks 3: its increment is $-0.83$ points $[-2.00,0.17]$, and $-0.83$ $[-2.00,0.33]$ with all three models at their lowest setting, neither significant. On the representative pairs in which exactly one point hits, a single comparison call names the right one in 85.9\% of calls, and the two orders agree on 71 of 78 pairs, 64 of them correctly; on the design items the single-call rate was 88\%. Most of the gain is in $F$. One default zoom of GPT-6.1 has no correct point on 82 items; three of them, at about the tier's cost, still have none on 62; the tier's three models have none on 43. Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage} names the rate behind this: on the 82 items that GPT-6.1 missed, a second GPT-6.1 zoom hits on 18\% and a third, after two misses, on 7.5\%, while Sonnet 5.5 hits on 29\% and Gemini 3.8 on 37\%, and Gemini 3.8 still hits on 26\% of the 58 items that both GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 missed. On these items, repeating GPT-6.1 adds little coverage, consistent with its persistent errors (Section~\ref{sec:res:persist}); the partners rescue more here, which Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage} does not guarantee in general. Counted with the same split, the three-zoom vote's 76 misses are 62 items with no correct candidate and 14 later losses, and the tier's 56 are 43 and 13: of the 20 fewer misses, 19 lie in candidate coverage. This describes the two outputs; it does not separate the roles of the models, settings and judge.

\paragraph{Items without an alarm.} On 499 of the 600 items the three points form one cluster and no comparison is made. The tier misses 29 of them (5.8\%), which is 52\% of its misses. An action applied to all of them, or through a trigger independent of whether the base misses, improves accuracy only if $h<e_T\,r/(1-e_T)$ with $e_T=5.8\%$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:switch}): about 3.1\% of the correct answers it touches if it repairs half of the misses. The judge's score alone misses 32 of the other 101 items (31.7\%), which is where the comparison acts. Nine clusters are chained, with two members farther apart than $\delta=1.5\%$, and none is wider than 2.13\% of the longer side.

\paragraph{Settings.} The default setting helps Sonnet 5.5 (misses $-4.00$ points $[-6.17,-1.83]$ against its lowest setting) and does not change GPT-6.1 significantly ($-0.67$ $[-2.67,1.33]$); on the design items the direction was the same. This is why the tier runs Sonnet 5.5 at its default setting.

\paragraph{The crop-first baseline errs elsewhere.} The crop-first baseline and the tier miss different items: both miss 42, only the baseline 27, only the tier 14. Of those 14, the baseline supplies a correct point on 7 items where all three of the tier's models miss, so it is a new source in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage}: added to the tier's menu, it lowers the items with no correct candidate from 43 to 36 ($\rho=7/43$). On the other 7 the tier had a correct candidate and lost it ($L$, 2 items) or chose wrongly ($W$, 5). Conversely, on 14 of the 27 items that only the baseline misses, its wrong point lies within $\delta$ of one of the tier's candidates, so adding it to the vote would mostly strengthen wrong clusters (on 12 of the 14 every nearby candidate is wrong).

\paragraph{What a fourth source can add.} On the 57 items where the crop-first point and the tier's point are more than $\delta$ apart, the crop-first point is right and the tier's wrong on 10, the reverse holds on 25, both are right on 12 and both wrong on 10. By Proposition~\ref{prop:switch}, a rule that switches between the two changes the number of misses by $-(10r-25h)$, where $r$ and $h$ are the shares of the 10 and of the 25 on which it takes the crop-first point. Within this two-candidate, threshold-triggered menu the largest possible improvement is 10 items (1.67 points). The comparison's accuracy on the representative pairs above does not determine $r$ and $h$ on these different pairs, so we evaluated an enlarged candidate menu directly. We added the crop-first point as a fourth candidate, ranked last among sources, scored by the same judge and handled by the same rules, on the same 600 items whose cross-tabulation above suggested it, so the result describes those items rather than new ones (717 further calls, \$1.44). Items with no correct candidate fall from 43 to 36, as Proposition~\ref{prop:coverage} gives with $\rho=7/43$. Lost representatives rise from 3 to 7 and wrong choices from 10 to 11: on 3 of the 7 items where only the crop-first point is correct, it joins a cluster of wrong points and loses the representative choice to a wrong member with the same score, and on one further item it displaces a correct member. The four-source tier misses 54 items against 56 ($-0.33$ points $[-1.17,0.34]$; 4 repaired, 2 broken) at \$0.041 per item (12.5 calls) instead of \$0.027. Proposition~\ref{prop:three} accounts for the result term by term: the added source pays in $F$ and is charged in $L$ and $W$. The tier of Algorithm~\ref{alg:clicks} therefore stays at three sources, and the crop-first baseline is reported as a baseline.

\subsection{Judge: selection has room left, but labels do not close it}\label{sec:res:select}

\paragraph{Selection among a model's own outputs.} Choosing the best feasible set from a model's own three calls would remove 45.2\% of set error relative to one call (median over 12 cells; Table~\ref{tab:select}), and 72\% to 74\% for GPT-6.1 on SKU-110K and DOTA. The constrained oracle differs from the unrestricted value on 25 of 720 images and has the same median. Record gates on output features, fitted with labels and evaluated by cross-fitting, reach 15.6\% (logistic) and 14.6\% (boosted trees); fitted on 10 labelled images they reach 13.2\%. The more flexible selector does not help under cross-fitting, and its in-sample value (36.5\%) is overfit. The empirical gap, median 26.1 points, combines information absent from the output features with selector and estimation shortfall (Proposition~\ref{prop:order}); our results bound it but do not apportion it. It measures the distance between the tested selectors and the best set in a fixed library of three calls, so it does not bound what adding calls or changing the library could gain. Within the operations we compared, the larger gains came from changing the information, a different view, model or readout, rather than from selecting among a model's own outputs.

\subsection{Judge: defaults that transfer}\label{sec:res:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} (full values in Table~\ref{tab:transfer_full}) evaluates construction transfer on stored reports: for each source, the construction that does best on the other five sources is applied to the stored calls of the sixth, so it is never chosen on the source it is evaluated on. Table~\ref{tab:defaults} shows where the final defaults follow these transferred constructions and where they differ. For set error, the best same-model construction on the other five sources is three-call unanimity with fused coordinates on every evaluated source. It lowers set error significantly in 9 of the 15 over-reporting cells and raises it significantly in none; on FSC-147, the under-reporting family, it is worse in 2 of 3 cells, consistent with Proposition~\ref{thm:intersect} and Corollary~\ref{cor:nonid}. For F1, the transferred same-model construction is better in 2 cells, worse in 4 and not significant in 12, so the default is a single call. Three calls cost three times one call. From logged token usage at list prices, unanimity costs \$0.029 to \$0.21 per image for GPT-6.1 across the five over-reporting sources, \$0.032 to \$0.13 for Sonnet 5.5 and \$0.011 to \$0.058 for Gemini 3.8.

\paragraph{Configurations that transfer.} The same logic applies to the configuration of a single call: the reporting interface (one list for the whole image, or one row for each region of a fixed registry) and the reasoning setting. For each model, we chose among the four combinations on three detection sources and applied the choice to the fourth, so the labels of the evaluated source are used only to score it (Table~\ref{tab:config}). For F1, the chosen configuration is better than the list interface at the lowest setting in 5 of 12 cells and worse in 1 (Sonnet 5.5 on DocLayNet, $-6.5$ points); macro F1 rises from 42.1 to 49.1, against 51.7 for the best configuration of each cell chosen with its own labels, and the chosen calls cost 1.44 times as much. For set error, the same rule is better in 2 cells, worse in 1 and not significant in 9 (median change 0\%), so the set-error defaults stay as in Table~\ref{tab:defaults}. One configuration shared by the three models transfers worse (F1: 5 cells better, 3 worse). Proposition~\ref{prop:approx} is the reason a choice made on related sources carries over when their risks are close, and the one worse cell shows where they are not. These numbers evaluate the selection procedure. The configuration that ships is chosen by the same rule on all four sources (GPT-6.1: regions, lowest; Sonnet 5.5: regions, default; Gemini 3.8: list, default; Table~\ref{tab:config_menu}); the folds exclude the labels of the source they are evaluated on, so they are not a replay of the shipped configuration on its own data, and the shipped choice coincides with the fold choice in 9 of the 12 cells.

When cross-model constructions are allowed, the transferred default improves set error in 12 of 18 cells and F1 in 10 of 18. It is better in 20 of the 24 cells in which the user's model is not the strongest single model for that source and loss, and not significantly different in the other 4. All 5 cells in which it is significantly worse are cells where the user's model is the strongest; for example, a three-model majority raises Sonnet 5.5's set error on GroundCUA by 52\% relative to one call, and a two-model intersection raises GPT-6.1's on FSC-147 by 58\%. Among constructions with averaged coordinates, against the best same-model construction chosen with all labels (a reference, not a deployable rule), the best cross-model construction is significantly better only on DOTA ($-5.8\%$) and significantly worse on GroundCUA ($+26.0\%$). When the user does not know which model is strongest, three-model unanimity is significantly better than three-call unanimity of a randomly chosen model on 4 of 5 over-reporting sources ($-4\%$ to $-11\%$). Voting a strong model together with weaker ones is not a safe default: a three-model majority is 52\% worse than GPT-6.1's own three-call majority on FSC-147 and 59\% worse than Sonnet 5.5's on GroundCUA, three-model click voting is 3.5 points below GPT-6.1 alone, and the per-type median of three models' dense counts is 0.60 worse than Sonnet 5.5 alone. On aerial and shelf detection the same vote helps instead: it is 9.5\% better than Sonnet 5.5's three-call majority on DOTA and 4.7\% better than Gemini 3.8's on SKU-110K; on DocLayNet and ScreenParse it is not significantly different. Cross-model confirmation is therefore a menu option chosen with a few labels or a family-level ranking, while the cross-model alarm is a default whenever a second model is available.
