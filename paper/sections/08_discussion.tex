\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a practitioner can take away.} The results reduce to a short list of diagnostics, each computable from outputs alone, and a fix with its failure condition (Table~\ref{tab:recipe}). Three quantities do most of the work. The readout gap, the difference between the list count and the stated count of one response, shows that the two disagree; the degeneracy flags then decide which to read, and by default the list count is read unless the list is degenerate. Agreement between two calls measures how stable the output is, not whether it is right; under the repeated-call model, high agreement means that another call with the same interface is expected to add little new coverage, while whether an additional call or source of evidence is worth paying for is decided by its measured benefit and cost in the evaluated configuration. The marginal matching rate $p_1$ of unconfirmed records says whether to delete or keep them; the shipped configuration encodes it as the task family's regime, and with three or more labelled images the label rule of Step 7 can switch to a construction that deletes or keeps.

\paragraph{Why the gains come from changing information.} The theory and the measurements point the same way. Object-level persistence means that repeated calls of one model share most of their errors. In our comparisons, votes over the same outputs gained at most 23\% for counts and 4.5\% for clicks, and even a perfect selector over three calls can only choose among the records those calls contain. The largest gains we measured came from operations that change what is available: reading a different field of the same response, looking at a different view, or asking a model with a different model-specific component. Where those operations fail, the failures have the predicted form: deletion hurts when $p_1>1/2$, tiling can lose objects that no tile contains whole, and a partner hurts when its additional loss of true matches outweighs its additional rejection of false records, with the effect of averaged coordinates counted separately.

\paragraph{Where click accuracy can still come from.} The split of Proposition~\ref{prop:three} locates the tier's remaining misses on the 600 new screenshots: 43 items with no correct candidate, 10 wrong choices and 3 lost representatives, and 29 of the 56 misses lie on items where the three models agree. A rule that chooses among existing candidates acts on at most the 13 misses of the last two terms. The other 43 need a source with a high rescue rate $\rho$ on exactly those items; the crop-first call has $\rho=7/43$, but on 4 of the 7 items where only its point is correct the tier still misses (on 3 its point loses the representative choice inside a cluster of wrong points), and on one further item it displaces a correct answer, so most of its gain moves into the other two terms. On items where the models agree, Proposition~\ref{prop:switch} gives the condition for any further action, $h<e_T\,r/(1-e_T)$ on the items it touches: if it acts on all of them, or through a trigger independent of whether the base misses, $e_T=5.8\%$, and an action that repairs half of its misses must break fewer than about 3.1\% of its correct answers. These two measurable quantities, the rescue rate on the items no candidate covers and the break rate on agreeing items, decide whether a further source or action raises accuracy, and both are measured from stored calls on labelled items.

\paragraph{What future models should change.} The measurements point to properties that model builders can target directly. A model's stated summary should be computed from its own enumeration: GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 already list almost exactly the right number of objects, and their stated counts lose that information. Long lists should not degrade with position; Gemini 3.8 at its lowest setting drops class fields more often when consecutive records share a class; an order that interleaves classes lowers its count error and grouping by class raises it (Section~\ref{sec:res:degen}). Class vocabularies given in the prompt should be respected, since Sonnet 5.5 still wrote ``diamond'' 258 times when the format named the allowed classes. Finally, errors that persist across calls limit what a fixed budget of resampling can repair; diversity across calls, or calibrated uncertainty at the object level, would make the cheaper same-model alarm as useful as a second model.

\paragraph{Scope of the results.} The theory's guarantees hold under their stated conditions. The identities of Section~\ref{sec:theory:identities} hold for any outputs and any fixed scorer. Proposition~\ref{prop:partner} assumes deletion-only confirmation, no match takeover, and no ignore region or none of the base report's false records inside one; outside these conditions the exact identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:identity} replaces it. The disagreement floor is a bound for point tasks and for boxes at IoU thresholds above 0.5; the click alarm is empirical and is evaluated by risk and coverage. Proposition~\ref{prop:nocert} concerns model classes that admit scenes with identical or nearly identical output laws; when an object is reported with probability at most $\vartheta$ per call, $K$ calls separate the two scenes by at most $K\vartheta$ in total variation. The readout repair applies where a readout gap exists: on our dense synthetic scenes the gap is most of the error, while on aerial and document images it is a small part of it for GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5. In all but three runs, changing the reasoning setting also changed the output cap, and Sonnet 5.5's reasoning share at its default setting ranged from 0\% to 90\% by task, so Sonnet 5.5 results are attributed to reasoning only where most calls reasoned. All results are for the model versions listed in Appendix~\ref{app:registry}, at a total cost of about \$758; we release every response so that the identities and tables can be recomputed for new versions without repeating our calls.

\paragraph{Broader impact.} Better-calibrated set reports can make inventory, document and screen-automation systems more reliable, and an explicit status and alarm make failures visible instead of silent. The same techniques could make automated agents act with more confidence; our pipeline always returns a status and never claims certification without labels, which we consider the appropriate default.
